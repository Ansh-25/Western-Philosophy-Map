Origins & the good life

Greek and Roman thinkers ask what the world is made of, how to reason and how to live.

Start here Start with Plato and Aristotle, then compare Stoic and Epicurean answers to what makes life good.

What is the world made of?

Milesian natural philosophyThalesc. 600 BCE

Ancient reports credit Thales with proposing water as the underlying principle of things.

The argument

Aristotle reports that Thales treated water as a fundamental source or principle. Aristotle conjectures that Thales may have noticed how nourishment and seeds depend on moisture. No writings by Thales survive, so his reasoning is uncertain.

Try it

Compare explaining a drought through the behavior of water with explaining it as a divine decision. What makes the first explanation open to investigation, even if the proposed theory is wrong?

Read Aristotle, Metaphysics I

Reality

Change and orderHeraclitusc. 500 BCE

The world is marked by change and tension, yet an intelligible order, the logos, runs through it.

The argument

Heraclitus pairs processes that appear opposed, such as waking and sleeping, to show how they belong to one intelligible pattern. His river imagery suggests that a thing can persist through the ongoing replacement of its parts.

Try it

A river keeps its course while new water flows through it. Would a city whose inhabitants all change over several generations remain the same city in the same sense?

Read Fragments

Reality

BeingParmenidesc. 480 BCE

A strict account of what is rules out coming into being from what is not, putting ordinary appearances of change under pressure.

The argument

Parmenides reasons that thought and speech must concern what is. If coming into being required something to arise from what is not, it would require us to make sense of nonbeing. His poem contrasts this argument with an account of the changing world mortals perceive.

Try it

A seed becomes a tree. Does that require something to come from nothing, or can it be explained as the transformation of what already exists? Later thinkers such as the atomists sought ways to preserve change without absolute creation.

Read On Nature

Reality

AtomismLeucippus & Democritus5th c. BCE

Bodies and their changes can be explained by indivisible atoms moving and rearranging in empty space.

The argument

The atomists distinguish stable constituents from changing combinations. Differences in shape, arrangement and position can account for the many things we encounter, while empty space makes motion possible.

Try it

Think of the same pieces forming different mosaics. The pattern is new even though the pieces survive. Could this kind of explanation account for felt qualities such as sweetness or pain?

Read Fragments and testimonies

Reality

Sophistic debateProtagoras5th c. BCE

His claim that a human being is the measure of things raises the question of whether truth depends on the perceiver.

The argument

Plato presents Protagoras as challenging the idea that there is a single appearance-independent answer to every question. Plato also explores whether even a relativist can prefer the judgment of an expert who makes outcomes better.

Try it

A breeze feels cold to one person and warm to another. Both reports may be valid descriptions of experience. Would the same reasoning work for claims about justice or arithmetic?

Read Plato, Theaetetus

Knowledge

What can reason teach us?

Socratic inquirySocrates399 BCE

Examining our beliefs about virtue exposes contradictions and helps us care for the kind of person we become.

The argument

In Plato’s dialogues, Socrates often asks someone to define a virtue and then tests that definition against examples and the person’s other commitments. The exchange can end in puzzlement, showing where further inquiry is needed.

Try it

If courage means never retreating, does a soldier who retreats to protect others lack courage? Try revising the definition so it explains both cases.

Read Plato, Apology

Ethics

Theory of FormsPlatoc. 380 BCE

Stable, intelligible Forms explain how we can know things such as justice even when particular examples are imperfect.

The argument

In the Republic, Plato distinguishes sensible particulars from intelligible objects such as Equality or the Good. The cave allegory depicts education as a reorientation of attention toward what makes appearances intelligible.

Try it

Two sticks can look equal from one angle and unequal from another. The standard of equality lets us ask which impression is correct. Does using that standard commit us to an independently existing Form?

Read Republic, Books VI–VII

Reality

Virtue ethics & syllogistic logicAristotle4th c. BCE

Flourishing is activity in accordance with virtue over a complete life. In logic, conclusions follow from premises by virtue of their form.

The argument

Virtues grow through practice and involve judgment about what a situation calls for. Aristotle also studies valid patterns of inference. In a syllogism, the arrangement of its terms guarantees the conclusion if the premises are true.

Try it

Courage may lie between rashness and cowardice, with the right response depending on the danger. By contrast, if every mammal breathes and every whale is a mammal, it follows that every whale breathes. Why does the first judgment require a different kind of reasoning?

Read Nicomachean Ethics and Prior Analytics

Ethics, Logic, Reality

What makes a life good?

CynicismDiogenes of Sinope4th c. BCE

A life of simplicity and self-sufficiency can expose the hold of status and social convention.

The argument

Stories about Diogenes portray him using public acts to challenge what people considered respectable. Reducing his needs was a way to depend less on wealth, rank and social approval.

Try it

If a promotion buys status but leaves you dependent on praise or luxury, has it made you freer? How much of Diogenes’s independence could a family or community reasonably practice?

Read Diogenes Laertius, Lives VI

Ethics

EpicureanismEpicurusc. 300 BCE

A pleasurable life depends on freedom from bodily pain and mental disturbance, cultivated through friendship and moderation.

The argument

Epicurus distinguishes natural and necessary desires from desires such as unlimited wealth or fame. Learning which desires can really be satisfied reduces anxiety. He also argues that death cannot be experienced as a harm by the person who dies.

Try it

A plain meal with friends may bring steadier satisfaction than a luxury that creates a new expense or worry. Which desires become easier to manage once you stop treating them as needs?

Read Letter to Menoeceus

Ethics

StoicismZeno, Epictetus & Marcus Aureliusc. 300 BCE onward

Virtue is the good that matters most. Focus your judgment and effort on what is up to you.

The argument

Epictetus distinguishes judgments and choices, which are up to us, from external outcomes such as reputation. Stoic practice involves pausing before treating an initial impression as a reliable verdict about what is good or bad.

Try it

Before an interview, you can prepare diligently and answer honestly. The hiring decision depends partly on others. What would it mean to take the outcome seriously without treating it as the measure of your character?

Read Epictetus, Enchiridion

Ethics

Where does inquiry lead?

Pyrrhonian skepticismSextus Empiricusc. 200 CE

When opposing arguments seem equally strong, suspend judgment and continue investigating.

The argument

Sextus describes the skeptic as still investigating. Where competing accounts seem equally persuasive, they withhold assent. Ordinary appearances, habits and practical needs can still guide everyday action.

Try it

You can drink honey that tastes sweet without settling whether sweetness is intrinsic to the honey. How far can you use that distinction in medicine or politics, where decisions cannot always wait?

Read Outlines of Pyrrhonism

Knowledge

NeoplatonismPlotinusc. 250 CE

Intellect and soul proceed from the One, an ultimate principle beyond ordinary being.

The argument

Plotinus places the One beyond even the distinction between thinker and object of thought. Intellect contains intelligible Forms, and Soul gives order to the sensible world. This dependence is a metaphysical order, not a series of events in time.

Try it

Think of many mathematical truths depending on a simpler principle without using it up. The analogy helps with dependence but leaves a puzzle. How can absolute unity give rise to multiplicity at all?

Read Enneads

Faith and the nature of existence

Christian PlatonismAugustinec. 400

Human restlessness reflects a desire for God. Evil is a deprivation of good rather than a substance of its own.

The argument

Augustine treats evil as a deficiency in something otherwise good. He also links wrongdoing to disordered love, in which a real but lesser good is prized above a more important one.

Try it

Blindness is a loss of a capacity in a being that ordinarily sees. Likewise, a betrayal can have real consequences even if its wrongness consists in a failure of loyalty. What kinds of suffering put this account under pressure?

Read Confessions, Books I and VII

Religion, Ethics

Essence and existenceAvicenna (Ibn Sina)c. 1020

What a contingent thing is does not explain that it exists. Contingent existence ultimately depends on a Necessary Existent.

The argument

Avicenna distinguishes the nature of a possible thing from its actual existence. Since its nature alone cannot make it exist, an explanation must appeal to a cause and ultimately to what exists necessarily in itself.

Try it

You can understand what a house is without finding a house on a particular plot. Existing houses call for further explanation. Does explaining every link in a chain explain why the chain exists?

Read The Healing: Metaphysics

Reality, Religion

Ontological argumentAnselm1077–78

Anselm argues that the greatest conceivable being cannot exist only in the understanding, since existing in reality would be greater.

The argument

Anselm starts with a concept of God and argues that supposing its object exists in thought alone leads to a contradiction. Gaunilo’s reply uses a seemingly parallel argument about a perfect island to challenge the inference.

Try it

Does defining the best possible island give you any reason to think it is real? Consider whether the idea of a greatest possible being works differently from the idea of a greatest island.

Read Proslogion

Reading, reasoning and revelation

Aristotelian commentaryAverroes (Ibn Rushd)c. 1179

Demonstrative philosophy can be legitimate within Islam, and apparent conflicts with scripture can call for interpretation.

The argument

Averroes argues that scripture can invite qualified readers to investigate nature through demonstration. Where an established result conflicts with a text’s apparent meaning, interpretive work can reconcile the two.

Try it

Imagine a literal reading that conflicts with a well-supported astronomical conclusion. What standard of proof should be met before giving up the literal reading?

Read Decisive Treatise

Religion, Knowledge

Negative theologyMaimonidesc. 1190

Because God exceeds ordinary categories, we can speak more safely about what God is not than ascribe human-like attributes to God.

The argument

Maimonides worries that familiar positive attributes can imply composition or human limitations if applied to God. Denying a limitation avoids some misleading claims while leaving room to speak about divine actions.

Try it

Calling God wise might suggest a person who learns and reasons. Saying God is free from ignorance avoids that picture. How much do we learn from a description framed by exclusions?

Read Guide for the Perplexed

Religion, Logic

Scholasticism & natural lawThomas Aquinasc. 1270

Reason can investigate some truths about God and discern basic goods that give human action moral direction.

The argument

Aquinas distinguishes truths reason can investigate from mysteries known through revelation. His natural law theory begins from practical reason’s grasp of basic goods, including life, knowledge and social living.

Try it

A law that makes travel safer can serve the common good. What if obeying it in an emergency puts a life at risk? Consider why practical judgment matters even when a rule has a good purpose.

Read Summa Theologiae

Religion, Ethics

The status of universals

NominalismWilliam of Ockhamc. 1320

Individual things exist outside the mind. General concepts can signify many individuals without a universal thing shared among them.

The argument

Ockham takes generality to lie in how terms and concepts signify. The concept of a dog applies to many animals without requiring an additional entity called dogness that is literally present in each.

Try it

A terrier and a greyhound differ considerably, yet both fall under the term dog. What makes such a grouping more than a convenient label we happen to use?

Read Summa Logicae

Renaissance challenges to authority

Political realismNiccolò Machiavelli1513

Rulers sometimes face a conflict between conventional virtue and the actions needed to maintain political power.

The argument

Machiavelli asks how rulers hold power in a world of unreliable allies, changing fortune and competing interests. A ruler’s capacity to adapt, which he calls virtù, can matter more than a fixed reputation for conventional goodness.

Try it

A leader has to decide whether to keep an agreement after an ally breaks it. What happens to trust if leaders regularly use necessity to excuse breaking promises?

Read The Prince

Politics, Ethics

Renaissance skepticismMichel de Montaigne1580

Attending to diverse customs and our own fallibility loosens the grip of confident judgments.

The argument

Montaigne turns observation inward, using his own changing habits and opinions as material for inquiry. Comparing societies reveals how easily local conventions can be mistaken for universal truths.

Try it

A visitor judges another society’s table manners while overlooking how strange their own customs seem to others. Which of your judgments would survive that reversal of perspective?

Read Essays

Knowledge, Ethics

New methods for finding truth

Experimental inquiryFrancis Bacon1620

Systematic observation and experiment can correct habitual errors and improve inquiry into nature.

The argument

Bacon calls recurring sources of error idols. They include tendencies common to humans, individual biases, misleading uses of language and inherited systems of thought. He proposes comparing cases systematically, including cases where an expected effect is absent.

Try it

If you think a soil additive improves growth, compare similar plants with and without it and record failures too. What would your investigation miss if you only collected successful cases?

Read Novum Organum

Knowledge, Science

Rationalism & dualismRené Descartes1641

Radical doubt leaves the existence of the thinker certain while they think. Mind and body are distinct kinds of substance.

The argument

Descartes tests beliefs by imagining reasons to doubt them, even the possibility of systematic deception. The act of doubting still requires a thinker. He later argues that thought and extension are distinct and asks how mind and body can form a union.

Try it

You might dream you are walking while your body lies still, yet the dreaming is itself an experience. Does certainty about the experience show that it could exist apart from the brain?

Read Meditations

Knowledge, Mind, Reality

Nature, mind and the state

Social contractThomas Hobbes1651

People authorize a sovereign to secure peace and protect themselves from the dangers of life without common authority.

The argument

In Hobbes’s state of nature, people lack a common authority to enforce agreements. Even those who prefer peace have reason to distrust others. The contract creates a sovereign capable of making cooperation safer.

Try it

Two neighbors both want to stop guarding their property at night, but each fears what happens if only one stops. What makes their agreement credible, and how much power should an enforcer receive?

Read Leviathan

Politics

Substance monismBaruch Spinoza1677

There is one substance, God or Nature. Understanding the causes of our emotions can make us more active and free.

The argument

For Spinoza, finite things are modes of one substance. Thought and extension are attributes through which we understand it. Freedom increases as we act from more adequate understanding of what moves us.

Try it

Understanding why a remark makes you angry may help you respond deliberately. If your new understanding also has causes, in what sense have you become freer?

Read Ethics

Reality, Religion, Ethics

Empiricism & liberalismJohn Locke1689–90

Ideas arise through sensation and reflection. Legitimate government rests on consent and protects natural rights.

The argument

Locke rejects innate speculative ideas and traces their materials to sensation and reflection on mental operations. In politics, people leave the state of nature partly to obtain impartial judgment and enforcement of rights.

Try it

You learn color through seeing and can form the idea of remembering by attending to your own mind. In a dispute over property, why might both sides prefer an independent judge to enforcing their claims themselves?

Read Essay Concerning Human Understanding and Second Treatise

Knowledge, Politics

ImmaterialismGeorge Berkeley1710

Ordinary objects consist of ideas perceived by minds. Berkeley rejects material substance as an explanation of them.

The argument

Berkeley argues that sensible qualities such as color and shape are known as perceptions. A stable world is possible because perceptions follow regular patterns sustained by God, rather than being invented at will by each observer.

Try it

A tree is still there when you leave the garden. Berkeley can appeal to divine perception and stable patterns of experience. Would this explain everything you expect the existence of the tree to explain?

Read Principles of Human Knowledge

Reality, Mind

Monads & sufficient reasonG. W. Leibniz1714

Reality consists fundamentally of simple substances, and there must be a sufficient reason why things are as they are.

The argument

Leibniz’s monads have no parts and express the universe from different perspectives. Their states correspond through a pre-established harmony. The principle of sufficient reason asks why this arrangement exists rather than another.

Try it

Imagine two clocks that always agree without influencing one another because they were set together. What would distinguish an underlying harmony from direct interaction?

Read Monadology

Rights & obligations

Debates about human nature and the limits of knowledge sit alongside disputes over sovereignty and equal standing.

Start here Compare Bentham and Kant on whether an action should be judged by its outcome or its guiding principle.

The limits of knowledge and freedom

Empiricism & inductionDavid Hume1739–48

Experience leads us to expect patterns to continue, but reasoning alone cannot establish that the future will resemble the past.

The argument

Hume divides relations of ideas, such as mathematical truths, from matters of fact. Inference beyond what we observe depends on expectations formed through repeated experience. Using past success to justify induction already assumes that past success is relevant to the future.

Try it

Bread has nourished you every day so far. What justifies expecting the next loaf to nourish you? Compare what you can prove with what you would sensibly do.

Read An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding

Knowledge, Science, Ethics

CompatibilismDavid Hume1748

Freedom can coexist with causal regularity. A person acts freely when they can act as they choose without external constraint.

The argument

Hume treats necessity in human action as the regular connection between motives and conduct. He defines liberty in terms of being able to act according to one’s will, so an action can have causes and still count as free.

Try it

Walking out of a room because you want to leave differs from being pushed out. Both events have causes. What changes when manipulation influences what you want in the first place?

Read An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section VIII

Mind, Ethics

Citizenship, duty and utility

Popular sovereigntyJean-Jacques Rousseau1762

Citizens can be free under laws they give themselves collectively when those laws express the common interest.

The argument

Rousseau distinguishes the general will, directed toward the common interest, from the sum of everyone’s private preferences. A law can express collective self-rule when citizens deliberate as equals about conditions that apply to them all.

Try it

A neighborhood votes on rules for shared water. Each household wants a private exception, yet all benefit from a reliable supply. What would help the group identify a genuinely common interest?

Read The Social Contract

Politics

Critical philosophy & dutyImmanuel Kant1781–85

Space, time and basic concepts structure possible experience. Morally, act on principles you could will as universal laws.

The argument

Kant asks which conditions make experience and objective judgment possible, including space, time and concepts such as causality. In ethics, he tests the maxim behind an action by asking whether it could serve as a law for everyone.

Try it

If everyone made promises with the intention of breaking them whenever convenient, the practice of relying on promises would collapse. What maxim best describes your action in a difficult case?

Read Critique of Pure Reason and Groundwork

Knowledge, Ethics, Reality

Classical utilitarianismJeremy Bentham1789

Judge actions and institutions by their tendencies to increase pleasure and reduce suffering for those affected.

The argument

Bentham proposes evaluating pleasures and pains through features such as intensity, duration and how many people are affected. This makes the effects of laws on ordinary people a direct part of political evaluation.

Try it

A transport policy saves many commuters a few minutes but imposes severe noise on a small neighborhood. How should the benefit to the many weigh against the burden on the few?

Read Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation

Ethics, Politics

Taste, equality and historical change

Aesthetic judgmentImmanuel Kant1790

A judgment of beauty arises from pleasure without a desire to possess the object, yet it asks others to agree.

The argument

In Kant’s account, judging something beautiful involves a pleasure independent of the wish to possess or use it. The judgment arises from subjective experience yet makes a claim on others’ agreement without applying a fixed rule for beauty.

Try it

You can admire a wildflower without wanting to pick it. When you say it is beautiful, what are you asking others to recognize that goes beyond reporting your own taste?

Read Critique of Judgment

Aesthetics

Feminist political philosophyMary Wollstonecraft1792

Women’s apparent dependence reflects social arrangements and unequal education. Equal access to education is essential to autonomy.

The argument

Wollstonecraft argues that education designed to win approval and dependence limits the development of reason and independence. This undercuts the claim that social inequality simply reflects natural differences in ability.

Try it

If girls are rewarded for pleasing others and boys for exercising judgment, later differences tell us something about training. What would count as a fair test of the capacities claimed to be innate?

Read A Vindication of the Rights of Woman

Politics, Ethics

German idealism & dialecticG. W. F. Hegel1807–21

Forms of thought and social life develop through tensions internal to them. Freedom takes shape in institutions and relations of recognition.

The argument

Hegel examines how a position can become unstable on its own terms and give rise to a more developed one. In the struggle for recognition, a master seeks confirmation from someone whose independent standing he denies.

Try it

Can a boss gain meaningful respect from workers who have no room to disagree? The example helps explain why Hegel ties freedom to forms of reciprocal recognition.

Read Phenomenology of Spirit and Philosophy of Right

Desire and commitment

Will & pessimismArthur Schopenhauer1818

The world as we experience it expresses a restless striving. Compassion and aesthetic contemplation offer ways to loosen its grip.

The argument

Schopenhauer interprets desire as the expression of a striving that continually renews itself. Satisfaction is often temporary, while aesthetic attention can suspend the pressure to pursue our immediate ends.

Try it

Achieving a goal can bring relief followed quickly by a new ambition. Compare that cycle with being absorbed in a piece of music. Are there kinds of striving that escape his diagnosis?

Read The World as Will and Representation

Reality, Ethics

Faith & subjectivitySøren Kierkegaard1843

Faith involves a personal commitment that objective argument alone cannot supply.

The argument

Through the pseudonymous author of Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard considers Abraham’s willingness to sacrifice Isaac. The story presses on the tension between obligations that can be publicly justified and an individual’s claimed relation to God.

Try it

Imagine a person insisting that faith requires an act others see as morally wrong. What could distinguish a genuine religious commitment from self-deception?

Read Fear and Trembling

Society, power and liberty

Historical materialismKarl Marx1845–67

How people organize production shapes social life. Under capitalism, workers produce more value than they receive in wages.

The argument

Marx asks how ownership and control of production shape class relations. His account of surplus value distinguishes the value workers create from the value of their labor power purchased in wages. He also examines how market exchange can hide social relations.

Try it

A coat appears on a shelf with a price tag. Trace back the labor, contracts and ownership behind it. Which of those relationships are visible in the act of buying it?

Read The German Ideology and Capital, Volume I

Politics, Ethics

Liberty & utilitarianismJohn Stuart Mill1859–61

Preventing harm to others can justify restricting liberty. In evaluating happiness, the quality of pleasures also matters.

The argument

Mill defends individuality and open discussion partly because they allow people to learn and correct errors. His harm principle restricts coercion of competent adults. In ethics, he distinguishes higher and lower pleasures by appealing to experienced judges.

Try it

A government proposes banning a book that offends many readers. Does offense alone justify a ban? What changes if publication exposes a private person to a concrete and foreseeable harm?

Read On Liberty and Utilitarianism

What ideas do and where values come from

PragmatismC. S. Peirce, William James & John Dewey1878 onward

Peirce clarifies ideas by their conceivable practical effects. James and Dewey develop distinct accounts of truth, experience and inquiry.

The argument

Peirce asks what observable effects would follow if a concept applied. James emphasizes how ideas are tested in experience, and Dewey sees inquiry as the work of transforming a troubling situation into one we can understand and act on.

Try it

If someone calls a material brittle, ask what difference that makes when it is bent, dropped or struck. Can differences in practice also clarify a disagreement about justice or meaning?

Read How to Make Our Ideas Clear and Pragmatism

Knowledge, Science

Genealogy of moralsFriedrich Nietzsche1887

Moral values have histories. Asking how they arose reveals the motives and conflicts behind judgments that can seem timeless.

The argument

Nietzsche traces competing ways of valuing, including how resentment can transform the virtues of the powerful into objects of condemnation. Genealogy examines what a value expresses and how it gained authority.

Try it

A community starts praising endurance under hardship. That value might express strength, solidarity or a need to make suffering bearable. Would learning its origin change whether you endorse it?

Read On the Genealogy of Morality

New methods for logic and experience

Formal logic & analytic philosophyGottlob Frege & Bertrand Russell1879–1905

Frege builds a logic of quantification. Russell shows how analyzing definite descriptions can reveal a sentence’s underlying logical structure.

The argument

Frege’s quantifiers distinguish claims about all things from claims about at least one thing and make complex inferences easier to inspect. Russell analyzes descriptions so their apparent grammatical subject need not refer to an actual object.

Try it

“The present king of France is bald” appears to name someone. Russell’s analysis makes it assert, among other things, that there is exactly one present king of France. Which part fails?

Read Begriffsschrift and On Denoting

Logic, Language

PhenomenologyEdmund Husserl1900–13

Careful description can show how things are given in experience and how consciousness is directed toward objects.

The argument

Husserl studies intentionality, the way experience is typically experience of something. The phenomenological reduction brackets ordinary assumptions about independent existence so that the structure of experience itself can be examined.

Try it

You see only part of a cup, yet experience it as a whole with a hidden side. How does the unseen side figure in what you are currently aware of?

Read Logical Investigations and Ideas I

Mind, Knowledge

Race & double consciousnessW. E. B. Du Bois1903 onward

Under racism, Black Americans confront the experience of seeing themselves through their own eyes and through a society that devalues them.

The argument

Du Bois uses the language of the veil and twoness to describe living as both American and Black in a racist society. Awareness of a hostile outside perspective can divide the self while also making social contradictions visible.

Try it

A student sees their ambition as ordinary but knows that teachers may read it through racial stereotypes. How might navigating both perspectives affect what the student knows about the institution?

Read The Souls of Black Folk

Politics, Mind

Pictures and propositionsLudwig Wittgenstein1921

A meaningful proposition can represent a possible state of affairs by sharing its logical form.

The argument

The early Wittgenstein treats the proposition as a model of a possible arrangement of objects. Its elements are related in a way that can correspond to their arrangement in reality, which lets the proposition be true or false.

Try it

A seating diagram represents people by showing an arrangement of names. How far can a similar model handle ordinary sentences about promises, wishes or obligations? Compare Wittgenstein’s later focus on use.

Read Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

Language, Logic

Logical empiricismRudolf Carnap & the Vienna Circle1920s–30s

Logical analysis and attention to empirical testing can clarify scientific claims and challenge traditional metaphysics.

The argument

Carnap and others used formal logic to clarify scientific language and relate claims to possible evidence. Their views developed over time as they faced questions about how theoretical entities and general laws can connect with observation.

Try it

An electron is not directly visible in the ordinary sense, yet a theory about it has testable consequences. How should a criterion of meaning handle claims whose links to evidence are indirect?

Read The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language

Science, Logic, Language

Being-in-the-worldMartin Heidegger1927

Human existence is already involved in a meaningful world of practices and relationships.

The argument

Heidegger argues that we usually encounter things through practical involvement. Tools matter within networks of uses, purposes and other people. Reflective observation is one way of encountering things within that broader involvement.

Try it

While hammering, your attention may be on the shelf you are building. When the hammer breaks, it becomes a conspicuous object. What does that change reveal about how equipment normally appears?

Read Being and Time

Science, freedom and the mind

FalsifiabilityKarl Popper1934

A scientific theory must take the risk of being contradicted by conceivable observations.

The argument

Popper distinguishes the ability to test a theory from the accumulation of favorable examples. A good test puts a theory at risk. A successful test can corroborate it provisionally while leaving future revision possible.

Try it

A claim that all swans are white can be contradicted by a black swan. What if a failed prediction could instead come from a faulty instrument or a mistaken auxiliary assumption?

Read The Logic of Scientific Discovery

Science, Knowledge

Existentialism & feminist thoughtJean-Paul Sartre & Simone de Beauvoir1943–49

Sartre argues that people make themselves through choices. Beauvoir shows how social conditions shape who can exercise freedom and how.

The argument

Sartre analyzes bad faith, in which people evade the responsibility of choosing by treating themselves as fixed roles. Beauvoir examines the material and social constraints that make freedom unequally available, including the treatment of women as the Other.

Try it

A worker says their role requires them to follow a rule they think is wrong. What choices actually remain open to them, and what costs would they face for refusing?

Read Existentialism Is a Humanism and The Second Sex

Ethics, Politics, Mind

The category mistakeGilbert Ryle1949

Treating the mind as a separate object alongside the body misclassifies concepts used to describe abilities and intelligent conduct.

The argument

Ryle objects that a mental concept can express a pattern of capacities and dispositions. Looking for a hidden thing that performs the work of intelligence can misread how such concepts operate.

Try it

A visitor sees the libraries and lecture halls and then asks where the university itself is. A similar mistake may occur when someone looks for cleverness as an extra event alongside a clever move.

Read The Concept of Mind

Mind, Language

Holism & naturalismW. V. O. Quine1951

Experience tests beliefs as an interconnected system. Quine also challenges a sharp divide between truths of meaning and truths of fact.

The argument

Quine argues that experience bears on networks of beliefs, so a failed prediction leaves some choice over which part to revise. His challenge to analyticity questions whether truths held fixed by meaning form a sharply separate class.

Try it

If a predicted eclipse fails to appear, you might revise the model, the timing or your assumptions about the telescope. What guides the choice when several revisions could fit the evidence?

For many words, meaning is understood by examining their use in shared practices.

The argument

The later Wittgenstein asks us to look at language-games, the activities in which words have a role. Some concepts are held together by overlapping similarities, which he calls family resemblances.

Chess, tag and solitaire are games despite differing in competition, physical activity and number of players. Can you find one feature they share, or does a pattern of overlap do the work?

Rawls’s original position is a thought experiment for choosing principles of justice. A veil of ignorance hides personal advantages. Equal basic liberties have priority, followed by fair equality of opportunity and the requirement that inequalities benefit the least advantaged.

A name can pick out the same individual in every possible situation in which that individual exists, even if our descriptions of them would change.

Kripke distinguishes how a name acquires its reference from what speakers believe about its bearer. Descriptions can help people identify someone, while the name can still refer to that person under counterfactual conditions in which the descriptions fail.

Justice in holdings depends on how property was acquired and transferred, and on correcting past injustices.

Nagel asks what it is like to have another creature’s experience. Chalmers asks why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all.

The argument

Nagel distinguishes objective descriptions from the subjective character of experience. Chalmers separates questions about functions such as attention and report from the question of why performing those functions feels like anything.

Prejudice can strip someone of credibility, and gaps in shared concepts can make certain experiences difficult to understand or communicate.

People involved in social processes that produce injustice share a forward-looking responsibility to change them.

The argument

Young distinguishes shared political responsibility for unjust processes from the liability of an individual who directly caused harm. People’s power, privilege and capacity to organize affect what they can do to change a system.

High rents can result from many ordinary decisions operating within the same housing rules. Who has the power to alter those rules, and what could people with less individual power do together?
